Help

How Gears works. Inspired by Matthias Wandel's gear work at woodgears.ca; for paper templates, his gear template generator at woodgears.ca/gear.

The teeth

  • Module is the tooth size: the pitch diameter is module × teeth, and the outside diameter two modules more. Diametral Pitch is the inch way (teeth per inch of pitch diameter); Circular Pitch is tooth to tooth.
  • The teeth are true involutes, cut as a rack would cut them, so any two gears of the same tooth size and pressure angle mesh. Small gears get the undercut the mating tooth needs.
  • The bottom of each tooth space is rounded to the bit, so a round end mill reaches all of it. That sets the smallest tooth a bit can cut; the Teeth card says what it is, and a bigger bit needs bigger teeth.
  • Backlash leaves play between the teeth; wood and plastic swell and shrink, so give them some. Tip Clearance is room at the root: blank is a quarter module, or more when the rounding the bit leaves at the root needs it, so the other gear’s tips clear it.

Cutting

  • Zero Z on the top of the stock; Total Depth goes a little past its thickness to cut through. Each gear's holes are cut first (bores cleared from the middle, so nothing comes loose), then its teeth from the outside.
  • Each pass ramps down along the path instead of plunging; Z never comes down faster than the Plunge Rate.
  • A bore is Round, Hex (sized across the flats: 12.7 mm for a 1/2" hex shaft), D (round with one flat, for a D shaft such as a motor's: Across the Flat is from the flat to the far side) or Keyed (a keyway Key Width wide, at least the bit's, Key Depth past the bore). Pick with the menu on its label; type a little over the shaft's size for a slip fit. A round bit can't cut a sharp corner, so each sharp corner gets a small notch for the shaft's or key's corner to sit in.
  • Lightening Holes or Spokes (the menu on the label): round holes in a ring, or that many straight spokes from the hub to the rim with windows between them, their corners rounded a little past the bit. Spoke Width blank picks about an eighth of the rim's radius.
  • Holes and Windows (Gear Bodies; Windows on the Gear Train tab, the same setting): Cut Out, the default, cuts round each lightening hole and spoke window only, with two small tabs holding the piece inside; knock it out and sand the tabs after. On big gears that’s far quicker (a clock train’s spoked wheels: about 20 minutes instead of an hour). Clear Out cuts all the wood inside away from the middle, so there’s nothing to knock out. Cut Out needs Holding Tabs on (their width and height); without them, and for holes under three bits across, the holes are cleared. Bores are always cleared.
  • Ring Teeth on Gear B's menu makes it a ring with its teeth inside, Gear A its pinion turning inside it, the same way. The ring needs at least 12 more teeth than the pinion at 20° (10 at 25°, 16 at 14.5°). It has a Rim Width outside its teeth, with screw holes (Gear B's Bore and Holes fields); it's cut from inside with tabs on its tooth tips, so the middle stays put, then round its outside.
  • Rack Teeth on Gear B's menu makes it a straight rack, Gear A its pinion: the same teeth, on a bar Rack Height deep, with screw holes (Gear B's Bore and Holes fields) along it, cut from outside with tabs on its bottom edge. The preview rolls the pinion along it; the facts say how far the rack goes a turn and where to mount it from the pinion's axle. Frame Holes is for two gears.
  • Gear B's label menu takes the Ratio A:B instead of its teeth (3 for 3:1): Gear B's teeth are worked out to the nearest whole tooth, with the real ratio shown.
  • Start from the Center Distance: type where the axles go, then the Ratio (how it should turn) or Gear A Outside Ø (how big Gear A is), and pick one of the pairs that mesh there: Coarse (fewest, biggest teeth: strongest, easiest to cut), Balanced or Fine (the most teeth the bit can cut: smoother). Each says its teeth, tooth size, pressure angle (20°, or 25° for a small gear when the bit allows), the ratio it really makes, the outside diameters and the biggest bit that cuts it. Typing in the card picks Balanced (or the kind you last tapped) straight away, filling in the Teeth card so the gears always mesh at that distance; tap another to change it. A blank Ratio is the one the teeth make now. Everything in the Teeth card can still be changed. A small gear’s teeth may be undercut (thin at the root): the list says so; a longer distance gives it more teeth.
  • Add a Gear to This Set (the same card’s title menu): a new gear that meshes with one you’ve cut. Gears mesh when they have the same tooth size and pressure angle; the center distance only follows from the teeth, so those two are kept from the Teeth card (Load the pair’s program first if you’ve changed them). Pick the gear you have in Meshes With and type where the new axle goes: only whole teeth fit, so it lists the nearest distances that work (each tooth moves it half a module) and picks the nearest. The new gear becomes Gear B (the one you have moves to Gear A with its bore and holes), and Cut is set to Gear B only.
  • Load and Save to File Manager (beside Generate): with the switch on, each program is also saved in File Manager’s Gears folder. Every program carries the settings it was made with, and Load fills the form from one, to cut a pair again or add a gear to it. The program’s first lines also say its tooth size and pressure angle. In the same panel, Save as a Set keeps the settings you have under a name (the program’s name to start with, selected so typing replaces it), without a program; tap a set to load it, or its bin to delete it (with Undo).
  • Drag the preview: the dot at Gear B’s center sets the Center Distance, the dot on top of Gear A its outside diameter (Start from the Center Distance). The value shows as you drag; the pairs are worked out when you let go, as if you’d typed it (in Add a Gear to This Set, the nearest distance whole teeth allow is picked). A tap on a dot goes to its field. Not for a rack or a ring.
  • Gear Train (its own tab): pairs in a row for more ratio than one pair makes, each stage’s driven gear on one axle with the next stage’s driver (glue or pin them together, or fix both to the shaft). Type the Ratio (turns of axle 1 for one turn of the last: 60 for 60:1) and it lists the trains that make it within Fewest and Most Teeth: the fewest stages first, more only when they make the biggest gear smaller (or, when no train makes it exactly, come at least twice as near); exactly when whole teeth allow, else within 0.5%. Every stage turns the way the whole train does: no speed-up inside a reduction. Or type the stages yourself, and Add a Stage or remove one. Drag the axles in the picture to fold the train round (or type each stage’s Angle; Fold Compact finds the smallest fold, Straighten puts them back in a line), start from a Preset, or tick Last Axle on the First for a same-center pair, as a clock’s hands (see Gear trains, plainly). Every gear is bored to the Axle Ø; the bigger gear of each stage gets Wheel Spokes. The preview turns the train and warns when a gear reaches another axle’s shaft (the frame’s holes aren’t drilled for such a train; the gears can still be cut). Cut all the gears in one program (packed in rows, biggest first, Gap Between apart), one stage’s two gears nested, one gear, or the frame’s axle holes where the layout puts them, axle 1 at X0 Y0 (with the Frame Holes tab’s depths). The tooth size, pressure angle, bit, feeds, depth, tabs and X0 Y0 are the Gear Pair tab’s.
  • Center Distance: blank is the gears' own. Type the distance the axles must be: a little wider spreads the gears to it (more backlash, which the facts show; too wide or too close is refused with the range that works), or press Fit Tooth Size to make the teeth mesh at exactly that distance. Frame Holes drills the axles at it. An idea: Spoilboard Toolbox's center-to-center spacing of your spoilboard's holes or threaded inserts is a distance to fit to, to try a pair on bolts in the spoilboard (for a test only: a bolt's thread wears a bore).
  • Click the Bit Diameter label to fill in the loaded tool's diameter from ncSender's tool table. With a tool changer, the label is a menu of the tool table instead: pick the tool to cut with, and the program starts with a tool change to it (M6 T). Typing another diameter, or No tool change, leaves it out. As in QuickCut.
  • Holding Tabs sit on tooth tips, where they're easy to sand off.
  • The diagrams follow what you type. Under the Teeth card, where the teeth meet, close up: a tooth of Gear B in a space of Gear A, their pitch circles touching at the pitch point, the line of action at the pressure angle, the backlash beside the tooth, the tip clearance under it, and the bit in A’s root. Under the Depth card, the cut from the side: Z0, the floor of each pass, a tab left standing, Total Depth into the wasteboard. Under the Cut card, the stock from above as the program will cut it, read back from the program itself: each cut through the stock the bit’s width, the holding tabs as orange dots, X0 Y0 in red, and the stock’s size.
  • Both Gears puts Gear B beside Gear A, turned so its teeth face Gear A’s spaces, Gap Between apart where they come closest, which saves stock. Blank, the gap is the least the bit allows: its diameter and a tenth, so the bit never cuts into the other gear. Make it more for room for a screw or clamp between the gears, or a sturdier strip for their tabs (with them that close, the tabs on the facing sides hold to very little). A rack or ring gear sits the same gap from Gear A. The Frame Holes tab drills the axles exactly the center distance apart; left blank, a hole is the gear's bore, or for a hex bore the hex's corners, so the shaft turns in it.
  • Programs are in mm; on an inch machine the program ends with G20 to put it back in inches.

Gear trains, plainly

  • Why a train: one pair can only turn so slowly before the big gear gets huge: 60:1 in one pair needs a 480-tooth wheel on an 8-tooth pinion. Split it into stages and the ratios multiply: 7.5 × 8 = 60 with gears of 60 and 64 teeth.
  • Stages and axles: each stage is a driver turning a driven gear. The driven gear is fixed to the next stage’s driver on the same axle (a compound gear: glue or pin them, or fix both to the shaft), so each stage sits in its own layer, one board’s thickness over the last. The picture numbers the axles; axle 1 is the one you turn.
  • Folding: the axles needn’t be in a line. Drag an axle round the one before it (or type its Angle) and the rest follow; the distance between meshing axles can’t change, only the direction. Fold Compact tries the turns in 30° steps (for up to four stages) and keeps the smallest box that fits, which may be the straight line. Gears in different layers can overlap; what they can’t do is hit another axle’s shaft, which runs through every layer: the facts say when one would.
  • Same center: a clock’s hour hand sits on the minute hand’s shaft. Two stages with the same teeth all told (driver plus driven) have the same center distance, so the second can fold straight back and land the last axle on the first. Its gear then turns on a tube (a pipe or bushing) over the first shaft, so it’s bored to the Tube Ø (Gear Bodies) instead of the Axle Ø. Last Axle on the First finds such pairs (the fewest teeth that make the ratio, exactly when they can, else within 0.5%); Frame Holes drills one hole for both.
  • Presets (Find a Train’s menu) set the ratio, the teeth and the layout for you: a clock’s seconds to minutes (60:1), its minutes to hours (12:1, same center), a slow crank (10:1), a spinner (1:5). Each says what the axles do. Change anything after.
  • Which way they turn: each stage turns the next axle the other way, so in a two-stage train the last axle turns the same way as the first; in one or three stages, the opposite way.

What to make with them

  • Clocks and automata: a wooden clock’s going train (the 60:1 preset) and motion work (the 12:1); crank-driven automata and figures that nod, walk or wave; a marble machine.
  • Replacement gears: a stripped gear in a hand-turned toy, game or model, or a knob-driven gadget, cut in HDPE, acrylic or hardwood (not in anything powered or holding weight up, such as a blind or a mixer). Count its teeth and measure across the tips: its module is that diameter in mm ÷ (teeth + 2), or its Diametral Pitch (teeth + 2) ÷ the diameter in inches. Most are 20°; match its bore with the Gear Bodies card, and use Add a Gear to This Set for its mate.
  • Workshop jigs: a gear as an indexing wheel (a 24- or 36-tooth gear and a pin or pawl divides a circle into equal parts, for fluting on a lathe or drilling a bolt circle); a crank with a small pinion for a fine adjuster on a fence or stop; a rack and pinion for a camera slider or a sliding stop.
  • Turning things slowly: a turntable for a display or for finishing a bowl, a rotisserie, a rotating sign: a slow crank, or a small motor on axle 1.
  • Planetary and ring gears: a ring and pinion for a compact reduction or a spinning display. For a planetary demonstrator, the ring’s teeth are the sun’s plus two planets’ (a 60 ring: a 12 sun and 24-tooth planets), all one tooth size; for three planets evenly spaced, the ring’s and the sun’s teeth together must divide by 3 (60 + 12 = 72: yes). Cut the ring and a planet on the Gear Pair tab with Ring Teeth, and the sun as a plain gear of the same tooth size.
  • Teaching and fun: a pair on a board to show ratios (Animate shows it too), a gear wall, a spinner or top launcher (the 1:5 preset), a coin-sorter’s drive, kinetic art.
  • And see the fine print: gears cut from wood or plastic aren’t for anything that could hurt someone if a tooth broke.

The fine print

  • You’re the machinist. Gears writes programs; you decide whether to run them. Check each one first: the preview, then ncSender’s visualizer, with the bit, the Total Depth and Z0 you’ll really use. Air-cut the first program on a new setup.
  • Safety is yours. Clamp the work well, wear eye and hearing protection, keep a hand near the E-stop, and stay with a running machine.
